### Choosing Between AI, Blockchain, Platforms and “Boring” Businesses

---

## Abstract

The previous papers compared AI infrastructure, blockchain platforms,
ride-hailing / delivery and FMCG businesses. This final paper distils
those insights into a practical **checklist** for founders and investors
deciding where to allocate scarce time and capital.

The guiding principle is simple:

> Prefer models where **real customers pay for real services** with
> understandable unit economics, and where long-run cash flows are more
> likely than not to exceed the cost of capital.

---

## 1. Step 1 – Is the activity real or mostly speculative?

1. **Identify the primary cash source.**
   - AI application: customers pay for productivity or quality gains.
   - Ride-hailing / delivery: customers pay for transport and logistics.
   - FMCG: customers buy physical products repeatedly.
   - Blockchain: ask honestly whether revenue is mostly trading fees,
     spreads and token issuance.

2. **Ask what would remain if token prices went sideways for 10 years.**
   - AI and FMCG businesses still have services and products.
   - Many crypto projects would see activity evaporate.

If most revenue depends on **price volatility of an asset rather than
service usage**, treat the model as speculative by default.

---

## 2. Step 2 – Unit economics

Regardless of sector, write down:

- price per unit $P$;
- variable cost per unit $c_v$;
- contribution margin per unit $m = P - c_v$;
- fixed costs per period $c_f$;
- expected volume $Q$.

Operating profit is:

$$
\Pi = (P - c_v)Q - c_f.
$$

Key questions:

1. **Is $m$ clearly positive today, without heroic assumptions?**
2. **How sensitive is $m$ to competition and regulation?**
3. **What happens to $\Pi$ if $P$ falls by 20% or costs rise by 20%?**

Sectors:

- AI applications: often have good margins if pricing reflects
  productivity value.
- AI infrastructure: margins depend heavily on utilisation and energy
  costs.
- Ride-hailing / delivery: $m$ is structurally limited by driver and
  customer constraints.
- Blockchain exchanges: $m$ can be high in booms but unstable.
- FMCG: $m$ tends to be steady and predictable.

---

## 3. Step 3 – Capital intensity and payback

Define:

- $K$ = capital required upfront (data centres, networks, factories);
- $CF_t$ = free cash flow in year $t$;
- $r$ = discount rate (cost of capital).

Net present value (NPV):

$$
\text{NPV} = -K + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + r)^t}.
$$

Payback period approximates the time until cumulative $CF_t$ exceeds $K$.

Rules of thumb:

- **AI infrastructure**: very high $K$, long payback, sensitive to
  utilisation and technology cycles.
- **Blockchain platforms**: lower physical $K$, but high intangible risk
  and regulatory uncertainty.
- **Ride-hailing / delivery**: moderate $K$ (software + operations),
  shorter payback if unit economics are tuned.
- **FMCG**: moderate $K$, long asset life, typically good payback if
  brands succeed.

If you do not have access to cheap capital and a long horizon, avoid
models with extremely high $K$ and uncertain payback.

---

## 4. Step 4 – Value capture and competitive position

Even when a technology creates value, not all of it accrues to the
company.

Ask:

1. **Who else wants a slice?**
   - workers, suppliers, app stores, regulators, token holders.
2. **How strong is your bargaining position?**
   - proprietary data, network effects, brand, regulation, IP.
3. **Can competitors copy your model easily?**

Examples:

- In AI, value may shift to those controlling **distribution and
  integration** rather than model training alone.
- In ride-hailing, multi-homing and local competition limit take-rates.
- In FMCG, strong brands and distribution networks can sustain pricing
  power for decades.
- In open blockchain, value easily leaks to tokens and forks.

Prefer models where you can articulate **why your firm earns durable
rents** that others cannot easily arbitrage away.

---

## 5. Step 5 – Regulatory and social licence

Regulation is often treated as a nuisance; economically it is a **core
parameter**.

Checklist:

- Is the activity likely to attract **financial-stability, consumer-
  protection or labour** regulation?
- Are governments supportive (e.g. productivity-enhancing AI) or
  suspicious (e.g. unregulated crypto lending)?
- Can your model survive if:
  - leverage is capped;
  - KYC/AML rules are strictly enforced;
  - workers are classified as employees?

AI faces scrutiny on safety and data protection, but policy documents
generally view **productive AI use** as desirable. Crypto-asset systems,
by contrast, are often viewed as **structurally fragile and speculative**
and are facing calls for containment.

---

## 6. Step 6 – Portfolio view

For an investor:

- treat AI infrastructure, ambitious platforms and some crypto plays as
  **high-beta options**: allocate only a portion of capital, expect high
  variance;
- treat FMCG and solid B2B services as **core holdings**: lower growth,
  but strong base for compounding and for funding more experimental
  bets.

For a founder:

- you only have one or two serious shots. Bias towards **real-economy
  problems with clear payers** rather than abstract narratives.
- AI is a strong candidate because it directly changes costs and
  quality. Blockchain may still be viable in narrow corridors (security,
  cross-border flows) but is far less general.

## 8. Conclusion

Across AI, blockchain, platforms and FMCG, the **same basic economics**
apply: unit margins, capital intensity, value capture and regulation.
Hype does not change these fundamentals.

The evidence assembled in this series suggests that:

- AI is building out a durable layer of infrastructure and applications,
  backed by large, persistent investment and real productivity effects.
- Public blockchain ecosystems are dominated by speculative trading, with
  a small but real pocket of payment and security use cases.
- Ride-hailing and delivery platforms provide valuable services but are
  likely to remain thin-margin.
- FMCG and food businesses quietly compound cash flows year after year.

Founders and investors who internalise these patterns can make more
disciplined bets: fewer narratives, more **economics on paper** before
committing capital.
