### Why Most Activity Still Looks Like Trading

---

## Abstract

Blockchain and crypto-asset ecosystems have generated episodes of
spectacular growth and collapse. Market capitalisation, trading volumes
and cross-border flows reach hundreds of billions of dollars per
quarter, and some exchanges post multi-billion-dollar profits in boom
years. At the same time, central banks and international organisations
repeatedly highlight that **most crypto activity remains self-referential
and speculative**, with limited links to the real economy.

This paper synthesises evidence from balance-of-payments style analyses,
central-bank and BIS reports, and exchange financials. It argues that:

1. The majority of crypto flows—especially in native tokens such as
   Bitcoin and Ether—are driven by **speculative trading, arbitrage and
   leverage**, not payments for goods and services.
2. There is a **small but non-trivial pocket of real utility**, mainly in
   cross-border payments and in censorship-resistant or high-inflation
   environments, and in some security / settlement use cases.
3. Structural flaws (high volatility, governance issues, weak consumer
   protection) and increasingly strict regulation make it unlikely that
   open, permissionless crypto will become a central pillar of the
   mainstream financial system.

In short: if AI is mostly about **using information to do work cheaper
and better**, current blockchain use is still mostly about **trading
claims on itself**.

## 1. What the data say about crypto usage

### 1.1 Self-referential activity

Analyses by central banks and the Bank for International Settlements
(BIS) describe the crypto ecosystem as largely **self-referential**:
activities mainly consist of trading one crypto-asset for another,
re-pledging collateral and leveraging positions inside the ecosystem,
with limited financing of real-economy activity.

Cross-country studies of exchange-app usage and trading volumes find
that:

- adoption and trading intensity correlate strongly with **risk appetite,
  speculative behaviour and macro-financial conditions**;
- flows spike when global funding conditions are easy and risk-free
  rates are low, and retrench when conditions tighten;
- transactional motives (remittances, payments) are visible but small
  compared with speculative flows.

In other words, the typical crypto transaction is **a bet on price
movements**, not a payment for goods, services or productive investment.

### 1.2 Cross-border crypto flows

Recent BIS-style analyses of cross-border flows show that:

- quarterly cross-border flows in major crypto-assets (Bitcoin, Ether
  and the largest stablecoins) have reached the order of **hundreds of
  billions of dollars**;
- a large share of these flows is associated with **trading and
  arbitrage** across exchanges and jurisdictions;
- real-economy use (for example, remittances) is more visible in
  specific corridors with high remittance costs or capital-account
  restrictions, but remains a minority.

Stablecoins and Bitcoin are increasingly used in some emerging markets
to move value across borders or hedge against inflation, but global
flows are still dominated by speculative capital.

## 2. Exchange economics: boom-and-bust profits

Centralised exchanges (CEXs) are the core business model of the current
crypto ecosystem. Public financials of a leading U.S. exchange show:

- **revenue** around USD 6–7 billion in strong years, roughly doubling
  from one year to the next when trading volumes surge;
- **net income** swinging from multi-billion-dollar profit in boom years
  to multi-billion-dollar loss in bust years;
- significant dependence on transaction revenue as opposed to recurring,
  subscription-like fees.

This profile—large positive earnings in good years, large losses in bad
years, and strong dependence on market sentiment—is typical of a
business whose main product is access to speculative trading.

### 2.1 A simple model of exchange profits

Let:

- $V$ = total trading volume per year (fiat-equivalent);
- $f$ = average fee rate as a share of $V$;
- $c_v$ = variable cost as a share of $V$ (payment, custody, network
  fees);
- $c_f$ = fixed cost per year (staff, technology, compliance);
- $T$ = value of token incentives and rewards per year.

Then revenue is:

$$
R = fV,
$$

costs are:

$$
C = c_v V + c_f + T,
$$

and operating profit is:

$$
\Pi = (f - c_v)V - c_f - T.
$$

Because $V$ is extremely sensitive to price volatility and speculative
interest, $\Pi$ can explode in boom years and collapse in downturns,
even if $f$ and $c_v$ are relatively stable.

Token incentives $T$ are often treated in marketing as “rewards” but
economically they behave like a **cash cost** that reduces value capture
for equity holders.

## 3. Real-world utility: 1% of the story?

### 3.1 Security and settlement

The original Bitcoin whitepaper and many early blockchain narratives
emphasised:

- censorship-resistant transactions,
- an immutable ledger of transfers,
- decentralised consensus without trusted third parties.

There are indeed **niche use cases** where these properties matter:

- transfers in high-inflation or capital-controlled economies;
- settlement in environments with weak institutions;
- specific security or timestamping applications.

However, for the majority of household and corporate payments in
advanced economies, existing systems offer:

- high reliability and low error rates;
- chargebacks and consumer protection;
- integration with credit, compliance and identity.

For those contexts, blockchain’s advantages are marginal, while its
disadvantages (latency, complexity, irreversible mistakes) are large.

### 3.2 Stablecoins and cross-border payments

Stablecoins—tokens backed by reserves in traditional assets—have seen
rapid growth in both market capitalisation and transaction volumes. They
are increasingly used:

- as trading collateral within crypto exchanges;
- for some cross-border transfers where traditional remittances are slow
  or expensive;
- in specific B2B and fintech contexts.

Yet central-bank assessments remain sceptical on their suitability as
**general-purpose money**: they highlight issues with legal claims on
reserves, redemption risk, governance and illicit-finance concerns.  
Put differently, stablecoins offer some efficiency gains in segments of
the cross-border payment market, but still fail many tests of reliable
money at scale.

### 3.3 Tokenisation without public crypto

Another emerging strand of research and experimentation separates
**tokenisation** from open, permissionless crypto-assets:

- central banks and banks test tokenised deposits and
  central-bank-digital-currency (CBDC) prototypes;
- securities markets experiment with tokenised bonds and equities on
  controlled platforms;
- unified-ledger concepts envision a programmable layer linking money
  and assets, not necessarily using public proof-of-work blockchains.

In these designs, the underlying idea—digital tokens representing
claims—is retained, but speculative public tokens may play a minor role
or no role at all.

## 4. Structural weaknesses of open crypto

The persistence of speculative activity relative to real-economy use is
not accidental. It follows from structural features:

1. **Volatility.**  
   Native crypto-assets exhibit extreme price volatility. This makes them
   unattractive as units of account and risky as stores of value.

2. **Governance and forks.**  
   Protocol changes, forks and governance disputes create legal and
   operational uncertainty.

3. **Security and operational risk.**  
   Hacks, smart-contract exploits and wallet-management errors are
   frequent and costly.

4. **Regulatory uncertainty.**  
   Classification as securities or derivatives, anti-money-laundering
   enforcement and tax treatment remain moving targets in many
   jurisdictions.

5. **Environmental and efficiency concerns.**  
   Energy-intensive consensus mechanisms and low transaction throughput
   limit scalability of some blockchains.

Because of these weaknesses, much of the risk is borne by retail
investors and traders. Institutions typically engage through tightly
controlled channels or focus on **blockchain-inspired infrastructures**
rather than public crypto.

## 5. Is blockchain “here to stay”?

Several distinctions are helpful:

- The idea of **tokenised records** and programmable settlement layers
  is clearly here to stay: central banks and financial-market
  infrastructures are actively exploring it.
- **Public, permissionless crypto-asset ecosystems** will likely persist
  as speculative markets, with pockets of real utility in cross-border
  flows and niche security contexts.
- The probability that open crypto largely replaces mainstream money and
  financial plumbing appears low under current evidence.

If AI is “real work with real customers buying real services”, current
crypto is still “mostly bets on future price moves of tokens”. That 1%
of security and payment utility may grow, but it has to overcome
structural and regulatory headwinds.

---

## 6. Conclusion

This paper’s central claim is not that blockchain technology is useless,
but that:

- **99% of observed activity** in current public crypto markets looks
  economically like trading, leverage and speculation;
- the remaining **1% of real utility**—mainly cross-border payments and
  some security use cases—is meaningful but narrow relative to the size
  of the ecosystem;
- institutional energy is shifting towards **tokenised versions of
  existing money and assets** rather than towards unregulated public
  tokens.

For founders and investors, the implication is stark:

- AI is already embedded in core production processes and backed by
  multi-trillion-dollar capex commitments.
- Public blockchain is, for now, primarily a trading venue with some
  useful side-corridors.
